Algorithms Do Not Decide.
On the Mislocation of
Agency in AI Systems.

The dominant discourse surrounding artificial intelligence systematically misattributes agency, judgment, and responsibility to computational processes. I argue this is not merely a rhetorical error — it has consequential implications for accountability structures, governance frameworks, and the philosophical foundations of AI ethics.

Central Thesis

Algorithms instantiate patterns derived from prior human decisions. When we attribute decision-making to an algorithm, we obscure the human agents whose choices are encoded in the system — thereby enabling a systematic evasion of accountability.

Argument Structure
01
Agency Requires Answerability

Moral agency is not merely causal efficacy — it requires the capacity to give and receive reasons, to be held answerable within a normative practice.

→
02
Algorithms Are Not Answerable

Algorithmic systems process inputs and generate outputs. They are not participants in the normative practices of accountability — they are instruments within those practices.

→
03
The Mislocation Problem

Attributing decisions to algorithms displaces accountability from the designers, data curators, and deploying institutions who bear genuine moral responsibility.

→
04
Structural Consequences

This displacement is not accidental — it is structurally incentivized by liability frameworks, organizational cultures, and the opacity of technical systems.
