FOUNDATIONAL PAPERS

01

Algorithms Do Not Decide

This paper argues that algorithms do not make decisions but generate outputs that become decisions only through human practices. By distinguishing outputs from judgments, the paper reframes the so-called “responsibility gap” as a category mistake grounded in the mislocation of responsibility within socio-technical systems. Drawing on Kant’s theory of judgment and Wittgenstein’s account of rule-following, the analysis shows that responsibility emerges not from computation itself but from institutional enactment and interpretation.

02

Authority Illusion

This paper introduces the concept of authority illusion to explain how responsibility becomes structurally displaced within socio-technical systems. While upstream actors define objectives, datasets, thresholds, and deployment conditions, accountability often concentrates downstream at operational interfaces. The paper develops an authority mapping framework showing how institutional structures obscure the relational distribution of responsibility across layered decision architectures.

KEYWORDS authority illusion distributed responsibility AI ethics institutional accountability human-in-the-loop

03

Responsibility Without Gaps

This paper develops a relational theory of responsibility for socio-technical systems by rejecting individualistic models of agency. Drawing on Wittgensteinian rule-following and institutional practices, responsibility is reconceived as distributed across interconnected roles, epistemic structures, and organizational forms of life. The so-called responsibility gap is therefore not an absence of responsibility, but a conceptual confusion regarding the nature of institutional action.

KEYWORDS relational responsibility distributed agency epistemic responsibility institutional practices social ontology
Read Paper → DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.19198110 Status: Under Review
04

The Responsibility Gap Is a Category Mistake

This paper argues that the so-called responsibility gap arises from a fundamental category mistake: responsibility is mislocated at the level of system outputs rather than within the practices that interpret and enact them. Integrating Kantian judgment, Wittgensteinian rule-following, authority illusion, and ethical withdrawal, the paper develops a unified framework in which the problem is not the absence of responsibility, but its systematic misrecognition within socio-technical practices.

KEYWORDS category mistake responsibility gap authority illusion ethical withdrawal systemic accountability
