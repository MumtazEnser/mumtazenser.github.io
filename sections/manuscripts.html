Organismic Humanism: Relational Ontology, Systemic Pathology, and the Conditions of Collective Survival
Minor Revision
Q1SSCI

This article introduces Organismic Humanism as a relational framework for understanding humanity as a collectively enacted form of life constituted through ethical, linguistic, economic, and institutional relations. Drawing on insights from Wittgenstein, Aristotle, and systems theory, it argues that contemporary crises — from ecological collapse to the expansion of artificial intelligence — are better understood as systemic pathologies rather than external disruptions. By reconceptualizing responsibility as a distributed yet structured phenomenon within relational systems, the article offers a novel theoretical framework for analyzing collective survival, governance, and coordination.

The Responsibility Gap Is a Category Mistake: Ethical Withdrawal and Artificial Intelligence
Under ReviewHakemde
Q1SSCI

This paper argues that debates on AI responsibility are conceptually misdirected. Drawing on Wittgenstein's account of rule-following, Strawson's analysis of reactive attitudes, and Sellars' distinction between the space of causes and the space of reasons, the paper develops a tripartite model of responsibility grounded in norm recognition, regulative capacity, and accountable sourcehood. The so-called responsibility gap is not generated by AI itself but by a strategic withdrawal of responsibility from human institutions and actors who design, deploy, and authorize such systems. Attributing responsibility to AI constitutes a grammatical confusion that risks enabling institutional evasion.

Keywords: Artificial Intelligence · Ethical Withdrawal · Strategic Withdrawal · Moral Responsibility · Normative Competence · Responsibility Gap

Ethical Withdrawal and the Delegation of Violence: Why Artificial Intelligence Does Not Decide — Humans Do
Under ConsiderationDeğerlendirmede Special Issue: AI, Judgment and Rules
Q1SSCI

This paper argues that claims about AI decision-making conceal an ethical failure: responsibility is displaced rather than automated. The most serious dangers associated with AI do not arise from machine intention or artificial moral agency, but from a distinctly human ethical phenomenon — ethical withdrawal. Drawing on Wittgenstein, Arendt, Weber, and Heidegger, the article diagnoses how ethical meaning becomes structurally distorted when moral discourse is translated into procedural form. If catastrophe occurs, it will not be because AI chose humanity as an enemy, but because humanity chose to abdicate the burden of deciding.

Keywords: Ethical Withdrawal · AI Ethics · Responsibility Gap · Passive Agent · Wittgenstein · Human Accountability

When Harm Becomes Inactionable: Ethical Withdrawal in Rule-Governed Practices
SubmittedGönderildi
Q1SSCI

This paper develops a Wittgensteinian account of a structural problem in rule-governed practices: the possibility that harm remains fully visible yet ceases to function as a reason for action. Drawing on Wittgenstein's analysis of rule-following, the paper argues that when procedural correctness substitutes for ethical judgment, harm is recognized but rendered normatively inactionable. The central claim is that contemporary ethical challenges arise not because harm is no longer visible, but because we have constructed systems in which its visibility no longer compels action.

Keywords: Ethical Withdrawal · Rule-Following · Wittgenstein · Normative Closure · Harm · Artificial Intelligence · Socio-Technical Systems

Ethical Antagonism: Ontology, Domination, and Accountability
Under ConsiderationDeğerlendirmede
Q1SSCI

This article develops the concept of ethical antagonism as an ontological feature of human relationality. Drawing on Hegel, Marx, Wittgenstein, and Arendt, the article rejects teleological accounts in which antagonism is ultimately resolved or sublated. The diffusion of responsibility within modern institutions entrenches domination through ethical withdrawal — the anonymization of agency that renders power unaccountable. Because antagonism is ineradicable yet historically transformable, the article proposes accountability to affected individuals as a minimal normative criterion.

Keywords: Ethical Antagonism · Ontology · Dependence · Domination · Accountability · Ethical Withdrawal · Hegel · Marx · Arendt · Wittgenstein

Critique as Diagnosis: Judgment, Temporality, and Rational Agency in Kant
Under ReviewHakemde
AHCI

This article advances a diagnostic reading of Kant's critical philosophy, proposing the three Critiques are best understood not primarily as normative foundations, but as a systematic exposition of structural disturbances in rational agency. Special attention is given to the ordering of the Critiques: the first Critique diagnoses epistemic disorientation; the second exposes structural acceleration of decision and action; the third thematizes judgment as a belated, retrospective faculty. By bracketing normative ambitions, the article shows that actions often precede judgment in modern agency.

Keywords: Critique · Diagnosis · Practical Reason · Judgment · Rational Agency · Kant

Wittgenstein and the Problem of System: The Possibility of Philosophy in an Already-Ordered World
Under ReviewHakemde
SSCI

This article argues that Wittgenstein's refusal to build a philosophical system should not be interpreted as anti-systematic, quietist, or merely therapeutic. Rather, it follows from a philosophical orientation toward the already-functioning order of the world — the grammatical givenness of reality. Drawing on the Tractatus, Philosophical Investigations, and On Certainty, the paper argues that philosophical error arises not from system-building itself, but from mistaking grammatical clarification for ontological discovery. Wittgenstein's philosophy is presented not as silence or theory, but as a realistic spirit of orientation.

Keywords: Wittgenstein · Description · Grammar · System · Realistic Spirit · Hinge Propositions · Normativity

The Conceptual Distinction Between Truth and Reality: A Methodological Proposal
Under ReviewHakemde
SSCI

This article analyzes how the ontological-epistemological distinction between truth (veritas) and reality (realitas) is systematically neglected in modern academic and policy-making practices. The paper proposes the Three-Phase Conceptual Delimitation Model, demonstrated through a case study on the conceptual gap between algorithmic fairness (policy truth) and measurable bias (social reality). The contemporary academic habitus actively suppresses intellectual originality by fostering an environment where paradigmatic consistency is valued above ontological discovery.

Authenticity as Deviation: Mimetic Repetition and the Formation of the Subject
Under ConsiderationDeğerlendirmede
Springer

This article challenges the assumption that authenticity should be understood as a position opposed to imitation. Drawing on René Girard, Jacques Lacan, and Wittgenstein, the paper argues that imitation is not a secondary distortion of identity but a constitutive condition of subject formation. The article proposes the concept of mimetic authenticity: authenticity does not arise from escaping imitation, but from the capacity to alter the enactment of repetitive patterns within mimetic systems. This becomes particularly visible in contemporary algorithmic environments and meme culture.

Keywords: Mimetic Authenticity · Mimesis · Repetition · Deviation · Subject Formation · Rule-Following · Algorithmic Culture

Persistent Disagreement and the Limits of Evidence: A Framework Account
SubmittedGönderildi April 2026
SSCI

This paper argues that a significant class of persistent disagreements does not arise from conflicts over beliefs or evidence, but from conflicts between what the paper calls synthetic grounds: background frameworks that structure in advance what counts as a problem, what qualifies as relevant evidence, and what can function as a satisfactory resolution. Drawing on Wittgenstein, Kuhn, and Sellars, the paper offers a diagnostic framework for explaining why disagreement can persist even under conditions of shared evidence and explicit rational engagement.

Keywords: Synthetic Grounds · Persistent Disagreement · Peer Disagreement · Deep Disagreement · Epistemic Grounding · Social Epistemology
